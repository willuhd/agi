A better school timetable

We built a program that makes the timetable for 626 students, 54 teachers and 63 rooms. Students walk about half as much, free periods stop piling up between lessons, a separate program checks every rule, and the plan still works when a room closes or a teacher is away. The timetable is the same every week.

A

What we were asked to do

a school of 626 students, 54 teachers and 63 rooms

The brief asks for three things: make a school timetable automatically, show that it is better than the current one, and prove that it follows every rule. We were given the course catalog, a model of the building and one real student’s week. Around those we built a simulated school, wrote a scheduler, and compared it with a stand-in for how timetables are made today. Every week uses the same timetable, so we plan one week and it repeats.

THE BRIEF · Question.txt, verbatim

The "schoo" has to produce a timetable that takes care of teachers, students, classrooms and courses all at once. This timetable affects the daily study and work of teachers and students, so having a high-quality timetable is important. We want you to consider the constraints in the school’s timetable and optimize the timetable. By this, you should design a system and be able to explain why it is better than what we currently have. An example of a constraint is class hours: you may change Math class with English class, but the hours spent on math class in a semester should not change. The result should be a system that automatically schedules timetables. There is no standard answer. How the current system works, which constraints must be satisfied, which aspects are worth improving: all of these are for you to research, judge and trade off, and you should explain your reasoning in your submission. During the presentation, the judges will ask why you made these decisions.

2. Evaluating Your Results

This problem cares more about which aspects you think can be improved and how you improve them. Your submission should answer:

• What problems in the current timetable system did you identify?

• Which aspects did you choose to optimize (for example, minimizing student movement between classrooms)? How did you optimize them?

• Who do these aspects affect, and how much? Why do they really matter?

• Compared with the current timetable, how much does your solution improve things? How do you measure it?

• How do you confirm that your timetable satisfies every constraint it must satisfy?

• Can your system still cope when circumstances change? How to present your results most convincingly is also up to you.

What is real, what is simulated

Real: the course catalog and its selection rules, the six-floor building with the walking time between any two rooms, and one student’s observed week.

Simulated: the 626 students and their course choices, the 54 teachers, and the “current method” we compare against. Everything is generated from a fixed seed, so anyone can re-run it and get the same numbers.

Fig. 2 — The 33 periods a class can use in a week, and how long each one is.

Why we count minutes, not lessons

A lesson in period 1 lasts 60 minutes, in period 2 only 40. Moving a Math lesson from one to the other keeps the lesson count but quietly removes 20 minutes. The brief says class hours must not change, so we count minutes.

Some periods are not available: PE takes Monday and Thursday period 5, and there is no Friday afternoon. This one grid repeats every week.

What is wrong with the current timetable

Question 1

In the one real week we have, a student changes room 21 times, because every teacher keeps their own room and students walk to them. Free periods end up scattered between lessons, and nobody checks that a lesson keeps the same number of minutes after it is moved. The three figures below show each problem in turn.

Monday of the one real week: 114 → 103 → 108 → 108 → 106 → 106 → 103
4 room changes in one day; 21 per week, 488 s of walking.
Fig. 3 — One student’s real Monday on the ground-floor plan: each arrow is a room change. Every teacher owns a room, so the student walks to the teacher all day.
Fig. 4 — Left: free choice. Right: students pick a package.

Why free choice breaks the timetable

If students can mix electives any way they like, each class shares students with many other classes, and all of those need different time slots. In our school that means 31 lessons that must not overlap, but only 29 free periods: no valid timetable exists. So we offer a limited set of course packages. Students in one package form a class that takes all its lessons together.

Class minutes drift without anyone noticing

Fig. 5 — Each class’s weekly minutes compared with the normal amount. A timetable built without looking at period length puts 110 of 270 classes outside the ±8% allowed band; ours puts none (the brief: class hours should not change).
  • Nothing aims to reduce students’ walking, free periods stuck between lessons, or overloaded days.
  • When a room is lost, the fix is made by hand.
  • There is no independent proof that the timetable follows the rules.
In short: lots of walking, wasted gaps, and no check on class hours.
C

How we built it

Question 2

We split the job into steps that can each be checked on their own. First, group students into classes. Second, choose when each class meets. Third, choose the room. Fourth, check every rule on the finished timetable and keep a quick way to repair it. The equations at the bottom are the exact rules and goals the computer works with.

Fig. 6 — The five steps, with the size of the problem at each one.
0 RulesCourse credits become weekly lessons. The selection rules decide which course packages are allowed.
1 ClassesStudents with the same package form a class of at most 24. A course needs at least 6 students to run.
2 TimeThe solver picks the period for every lesson, obeying all hard rules and cutting idle gaps. It uses one yes/no variable per class and period, so it stays fast.
3 RoomsEach class gets a home room. Leftover lessons are matched to free rooms, then swapped to cut walking.
4 CheckA separate program checks the ten rules. If something changes, only the affected lessons are re-planned.
Fig. 7 — How many lessons a week a course gets: catalog credit times a factor fitted on the real week, tried across a whole band of values.

Credit to weekly slots

$$n_c=\Big\lfloor k\kappa_c+\tfrac12\Big\rfloor,\quad k=\frac{\sum_i\kappa_i o_i}{\sum_i\kappa_i^{2}}\approx 0.873$$

Hard rules (zg,s ∈ {0,1})

$$\sum_s z_{g,s}=n_g,\quad \sum_{g\in\sigma}z_{g,s}\le1,\quad \sum_{g\in T_t}z_{g,s}\le1$$$$\sum_{g:\,a_g\ge a}z_{g,s}\le R_\kappa(a)\quad(\text{room area})$$

Contact hours in minutes

$$\Big|\sum_s\mu_s z_{g,s}-n_g\bar\mu\Big|\le\max(\tau n_g\bar\mu,\,9),\ \tau=0.08$$

Stage 1: slot objective

$$\min\ w_{gap}\sum_\sigma c_\sigma\Gamma_\sigma+w_h\sum_\sigma c_\sigma H_\sigma+w_{sp}\sum_g|g|\,\mathrm{rep}_g+w_m\!\!\sum_{g\ \mathrm{hard}}\!\!|g|\sum_s(p_s-1)z_{g,s}+w_{tg}\sum_t\Gamma_t$$

Stage 2: person-seconds

$$\min_r\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal L_{stud}}n_{ab}\,\tau(r_a,r_b)+\lambda\!\!\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal L_{teach}}\!\!\tau(r_a,r_b)$$

Score, fairness, robustness

$$J=\bar\ell_{stud}+\alpha\bar L_t+\beta\,\mathrm{CVaR}_q(L),\qquad \mathbb E[\Delta]=\sum_\xi p_\xi\Delta(\xi)$$

Students stay, teachers walk: λ says how much one second of teacher walking counts against one second of student walking. Γ = free periods stuck between lessons, H = days with six or more classes, rep = a class meeting twice on one day.

In plain words: every class gets exactly its number of lessons; nobody (student, teacher or room) is in two places at once; weekly minutes stay near the normal amount; then the computer removes as many wasted gaps and as much walking as it can.

How we know the rules are followed

Question 5

The ten rules in plain words

  • Every class gets all its lessons, in different periods.
  • Lessons avoid PE time and the teacher’s other commitments.
  • No student, teacher or room is in two places at once.
  • The room is the right kind and big enough; the teacher is qualified.
  • Teachers never teach more in a row than their limit; labs get a double period.
  • Weekly minutes stay near the normal amount.
  • Every room change fits in the break between lessons.

20 automated tests also cover loads, course rules, the solver, repairs and the real week.

F

What if our assumptions are wrong?

Question 6

Many numbers in a simulation are assumptions, so we changed each one, first one at a time and then several together, and re-ran everything. The gain stays in a narrow band in almost every case. The exceptions are real limits: a much heavier timetable, or losing a lot of rooms, can leave no valid timetable at all.

What the sweeps tell us

  • Across every single-assumption change, the walking saving stays between 45% and 58%.
  • The gain holds up against the importance weights, class size limits, staffing and the solver random seed.
  • It does not hold up against the load: a heavier timetable (k near 1) or losing 40% of rooms leaves no valid timetable, for the current method as well as ours.
  • Offering more course packages costs more classes to staff.
G

What happens when something breaks?

Question 6

Rooms close, teachers fall ill and enrollment grows. When that happens the program changes as little as it can: first it looks for a substitute, then it moves a lesson to another room, and only as a last step does it change a lesson’s time. Every repaired timetable is checked again.

Fig. 25 — 28 lessons lose their room; 28 are re-roomed in a free room of the same kind, 0 lessons change time. Blue = receiving rooms.
Fig. 26 — A teacher is away for a day (probability 0.4). A qualified free colleague covers 87% of lessons and nothing in the timetable moves.
Fig. 27 — Enrollment grows 5% (probability 0.3). Rooms that become too small are swapped for bigger ones; lesson times stay the same.

The repair ladder

  • 1. Substitute. A qualified teacher who is free at that moment takes the lesson.
  • 2. Move the room. The lesson keeps its time and moves to a free room of the right kind and size.
  • 3. Move the time. Only if no room is free: just the affected classes are re-planned, everything else stays frozen.

Over 12 random draws of each event, no repair was impossible and no lesson had to change its time. Expected cost: 0.00 lessons moved and 14.4 room changes per event.

A closed room, a sick teacher or a growing intake is handled by small local changes, not a new timetable.
H

What we have not proven, and how to re-run it

limits

  • One real week only. The current system is an emulator calibrated to it (about 20 vs 21 changes); everything else is simulated. Registrar CSVs drop in.
  • Operating envelope. Valid timetables were found at k ≈ 0.87 with up to 20% of rooms removed; at k ≥ 0.95 or ≥ 40% rooms removed none was found in the time limit (Fig. 20).
  • Not proven optimal. CP-SAT stopped at its time limit, 78% from its bound: “optimized” means best feasible found.
  • Pathway classes trade choice for feasibility (Fig. 4); the price is quantified in F.
  • Minutes: sections of one course can still differ by up to 36% in weekly minutes; the next rule to add is equality across sections.
  • Reproduce: python -m tt.run, python -m tt.sensitivity, python -m tt.poster2.
  • Every number on this poster is read from output/*.json; seeds and the config hash are fixed.
