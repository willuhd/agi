A

Problem Statement and Scope

626 students, 54 teachers, 59 rooms

The brief requires an automatic school timetable, evidence that it improves on the current one, and verification that it satisfies every constraint. The inputs are the course catalog, a model of the building and one observed student week. A simulated school of 626 students (grades 9, 10 and 11 AP) is constructed from these inputs, scheduled automatically and compared with an emulation of the current method. The timetable is identical in every week; one week is planned and repeated.

BRIEF · Question.txt, verbatim

The school has to produce a timetable that takes care of teachers, students, classrooms and courses all at once. This timetable affects the daily study and work of teachers and students, so having a high-quality timetable is important. We want you to consider the constraints in the school’s timetable and optimize the timetable. By this, you should design a system and be able to explain why it is better than what we currently have. An example of a constraint is class hours: you may change Math class with English class, but the hours spent on math class in a semester should not change. The result should be a system that automatically schedules timetables. There is no standard answer. How the current system works, which constraints must be satisfied, which aspects are worth improving: all of these are for you to research, judge and trade off, and you should explain your reasoning in your submission. During the presentation, the judges will ask why you made these decisions.

2. Evaluating Your Results

This problem cares more about which aspects you think can be improved and how you improve them. Your submission should answer:

• What problems in the current timetable system did you identify?

• Which aspects did you choose to optimize (for example, minimizing student movement between classrooms)? How did you optimize them?

• Who do these aspects affect, and how much? Why do they really matter?

• Compared with the current timetable, how much does your solution improve things? How do you measure it?

• How do you confirm that your timetable satisfies every constraint it must satisfy?

• Can your system still cope when circumstances change? How to present your results most convincingly is also up to you.

Data Sources and Simulation Scope

Real inputs: the course catalog and its selection rules, the building model with walking times between rooms, and one observed student week.

Simulated components: the 626 students and their course choices, the 54 teachers, and the emulated current method. All components are generated from a fixed random seed and are reproducible.

Fig. 2 — The 33 periods available per week and their lengths.

Contact Hours Measured in Minutes

Period 1 lasts 60 minutes and period 2 lasts 40. Moving a lesson between them preserves the lesson count but changes the weekly contact time by 20 minutes. Because the brief requires class hours to remain unchanged, contact time is counted in minutes.

Some periods are unavailable: PE occupies Monday and Thursday period 5, and Friday has no afternoon periods. The same grid applies in every week.

Assumption on Rooms

Floor B2 is used for club activities only and hosts no classes. It is removed from the room pool, which also removes all 14 laboratories: the sciences meet in ordinary classrooms and keep their practical double period. The pool is 49 classrooms (floors 1F to 4F) and 10 arts rooms (B1). The checker rejects any lesson placed on B2.

Effect of Free Elective Choice on Feasibility

When students combine electives freely, each class shares students with many others, and all such classes require distinct time slots. In the simulated school this produces 31 mutually exclusive lessons for 29 free periods, so no valid timetable exists. A limited set of course packages is therefore offered; students in one package form a single class that takes all of its lessons together.

C

Method

Question 2

The method is divided into stages that can each be verified separately: grouping students into classes, assigning each class to periods, assigning rooms, and verifying all constraints on the finished timetable, with a local repair procedure. The equations below state the constraints and objectives used.

Fig. 6 — The five stages and the size of the problem at each.
0 RulesCourse credits are converted into weekly lessons. Selection rules determine the permitted course packages.
1 ClassesStudents with the same package form one class of at most 24. A course runs only with at least 6 students.
2 TimeA constraint solver assigns a period to every lesson, subject to all hard constraints, while minimizing idle gaps. One binary variable per class and period keeps the model compact.
3 RoomsEach class receives a home room. Remaining lessons are matched to free rooms and swapped to reduce walking.
4 CheckAn independent program checks the ten constraints. After a change, only the affected lessons are re-planned.
Fig. 7 — Weekly lessons per course: catalog credit multiplied by a factor fitted on the observed week, evaluated over a band of values.

Credit to weekly slots

$$n_c=\Big\lfloor k\kappa_c+\tfrac12\Big\rfloor,\quad k=\frac{\sum_i\kappa_i o_i}{\sum_i\kappa_i^{2}}\approx 0.873$$

Hard constraints (zg,s ∈ {0,1})

$$\sum_s z_{g,s}=n_g,\quad \sum_{g\in\sigma}z_{g,s}\le1,\quad \sum_{g\in T_t}z_{g,s}\le1$$$$\sum_{g:\,a_g\ge a}z_{g,s}\le R_\kappa(a)\quad(\text{room area})$$

Contact hours in minutes

$$\Big|\sum_s\mu_s z_{g,s}-n_g\bar\mu\Big|\le\max(\tau n_g\bar\mu,\,9),\ \tau=0.08$$

Stage 1: slot objective

$$\begin{aligned}\min\ &w_{gap}\sum_\sigma c_\sigma\Gamma_\sigma+w_h\sum_\sigma c_\sigma H_\sigma+w_{sp}\sum_g|g|\,\mathrm{rep}_g\\ &+w_m\!\!\sum_{g\ \mathrm{hard}}\!\!|g|\sum_s(p_s-1)z_{g,s}+w_{tg}\sum_t\Gamma_t\end{aligned}$$

Stage 2: person-seconds

$$\min_r\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal L_{stud}}n_{ab}\,\tau(r_a,r_b)+\lambda\!\!\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal L_{teach}}\!\!\tau(r_a,r_b)$$

Score, fairness, robustness

$$J=\bar\ell_{stud}+\alpha\bar L_t+\beta\,\mathrm{CVaR}_q(L),\qquad \mathbb E[\Delta]=\sum_\xi p_\xi\Delta(\xi)$$

Students remain in their rooms and teachers move: λ is the weight of one second of teacher walking relative to one second of student walking. Γ = idle periods between lessons, H = days with six or more classes, rep = a class meeting twice on one day.

Summary of the constraints and objectives: each class receives exactly its number of lessons; no student, teacher or room is assigned to two places at once; weekly minutes remain near the nominal amount; idle gaps and walking are then minimized.

The Ten Hard Constraints

  • Every class receives all its weekly lessons, in distinct periods.
  • Lessons avoid PE time and the teacher’s external commitments.
  • No student, teacher or room is assigned twice in one period.
  • The room has the correct type and sufficient area; the teacher is qualified for the department.
  • Teachers do not exceed their consecutive-period limit; practical (science) classes receive a double period.
  • Weekly minutes remain within tolerance of the nominal amount.
  • Every room change is reachable within the break between lessons.

20 automated tests additionally cover loads, course rules, the solver, repairs and the observed week.

F

Sensitivity Analysis

Question 6

Several inputs of the simulation are assumptions. Each was varied individually and then several together, and the full pipeline was re-run. The walking reduction remains within a narrow band in nearly all cases. The exceptions are genuine limits: a much heavier timetable, or the loss of many rooms, can leave no valid timetable.

Fig. 19 — Loss of rooms: valid timetables were found with up to 20% of rooms removed and none with 40% or more; the program reports infeasibility instead of an invalid timetable.
Fig. 20 — Operating envelope. Each marker is one run; the timetable load (k) and the share of rooms lost determine whether a valid timetable is found.
Fig. 21 — Increasing the weight on idle gaps reduces them smoothly, without a discontinuity.
Fig. 22 — Teachers required as supply tightens.

Summary of Sensitivity Findings

  • The walking reduction remains between 78% and 83% across all single-assumption changes.
  • It is robust to the objective weights, class-size limits, staffing and the solver random seed.
  • It is not robust to timetable load: a heavier load (k close to 1) or the loss of 40% of rooms leaves no valid timetable, for the current method as well as the optimized one.
  • Offering more course packages increases the number of classes to staff.
G

Robustness and Repair

Question 6

When a room closes, a teacher is absent or enrollment grows, the system applies the smallest possible change: it first seeks a substitute teacher, then moves the lesson to another room, and changes a lesson’s time only as a last resort. Every repaired timetable is checked again.

Fig. 25 — 27 lessons lose their room; 27 are re-roomed in a free room of the same kind, 0 lessons change time. Blue = receiving rooms.
Fig. 26 — A teacher is absent for one day (probability 0.4). A qualified free colleague covers 85% of lessons; no lesson moves.
Fig. 27 — Enrollment rises 5% (probability 0.3). Rooms that become too small are replaced by larger ones; lesson times are unchanged.

Repair Procedure

  • 1. Substitution. A qualified teacher who is free in that period takes the lesson.
  • 2. Room change. The lesson keeps its period and moves to a free room of the correct type and size.
  • 3. Time change. Only if no room is free, the affected classes are re-planned while all other lessons stay fixed.

Over 12 random draws of each event, every repair was possible and no lesson required a change of time. Expected cost per event: 0.00 lessons moved and 20.6 room changes.

Summary: a closed room, an absent teacher or higher enrollment is handled by local changes, without rebuilding the timetable.
H

Limitations and Reproducibility

scope of the evidence

  • Room assumption. Floor B2 (club use only, including all laboratories) is excluded; sciences meet in classrooms.
  • One real week only. The current system is an emulator calibrated to it (about 18 vs 21 changes); everything else is simulated. Registrar data can replace the simulated inputs.
  • Operating envelope. Valid timetables were found up to k = 0.87 and with up to 20% of rooms removed; beyond k = 0.95 or 40% of rooms removed none was found in the time limit (Fig. 20).
  • Not proven optimal. CP-SAT stopped at its time limit, 93% from its bound: “optimized” means best feasible found.
  • Pathway classes trade choice for feasibility (Fig. 4); the price is quantified in F.
  • Minutes: sections of one course can still differ by up to 36% in weekly minutes; the next rule to add is equality across sections.
  • Reproduce: python -m run, python -m sensitivity, python -m poster2.
  • Every number on this poster is read from output/*.json; seeds and the config hash are fixed.
